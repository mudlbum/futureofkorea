Korea has spent most of the last two decades running one of the more restrictive skilled-migration systems in the OECD. In 2026 that changed noticeably — not because of a change of heart, but because of arithmetic. The working-age population is shrinking, regional cities are emptying, and manufacturers outside the capital region cannot fill technical roles.

The result is a set of reforms that genuinely widen the door. Here is what each one is and who it is for.

> [!WARNING] Verify everything here before you act
> Korean visa rules change frequently, sometimes mid-year, and officers apply current internal guidance rather than what a website said last quarter. Everything below is accurate to the best of our research as of **12 August 2026** and linked to its source. Before you file anything, confirm with **HiKorea** or the **1345 contact centre**. This is not immigration advice — see the [disclaimer](/disclaimer/).

## 1. Top-Tier visa — now open to academics, and unusually generous on family

The Top-Tier route is Korea's answer to the global competition for elite talent. Its **expansion to science and technology professors and researchers took effect in June 2026**.

**Eligibility** is built around a small number of high bars, of which you need to satisfy the specified combination:

- Annual income of at least **three times Korea's per-capita GNI**
- A **master's degree from a top-100 university**
- Experience at a **Global 500 company**

**What makes it worth the effort** is not the work permission — it is the family scope. Top-Tier holders can reportedly bring **spouse, minor children, parents, and a full-time domestic helper**. In a system where dependent categories are normally tight, extending status to parents is a substantial policy departure, and for mid-career professionals with ageing parents it can be the deciding factor between Korea and Singapore or Japan.

> [!TIP] The income threshold moves every year
> "3× per-capita GNI" is not a fixed won figure. GNI is published annually by the Bank of Korea, so the threshold drifts. Never plan against a number you read in an article — including this one. Pull the current GNI from the Bank of Korea and multiply, then confirm with 1345.

## 2. E-7-1 — the experience requirement just got waivable

The **E-7-1 special occupation visa** is the standard professional work visa: you have an employer, a job offer, and a role that maps to a designated occupation code.

Its most common blocker for recent graduates was the **one-year work-experience requirement**. In 2026, that requirement is waived for **science and engineering majors who complete a designated education programme run by the Korea Chamber of Commerce and Industry**.

This is a bigger deal than it sounds. It converts E-7-1 from a visa for experienced hires into one a strong new graduate can realistically obtain — which in turn changes what Korean employers can offer international candidates at entry level.

## 3. E-7-M ("K-CORE") — a manufacturing route with a residence pathway

**E-7-M**, branded **K-CORE**, is new and narrowly targeted: it is for people who graduate from **designated Korean junior-college technical programmes in manufacturing-related fields**.

It offers a work-visa pathway *and* an onward route to long-term residence. Read that as deliberate policy design: Korea is not trying to import rotating short-term labour into its industrial base, it is trying to grow people into it. If you are considering technical study in Korea, the existence of a defined graduate-to-residence pipeline materially changes the value of a two-year programme.

## 4. F-2-R — the regional track, with a lower bar

**F-2-7** is Korea's points-based residence visa: accumulate points for age, education, income, Korean language ability, time in Korea and other factors, clear the threshold, and you get residence status that is not tied to an employer.

**F-2-R** is its regional sibling. It is calibrated for employers in **depopulated and industrial regions outside the major metropolitan areas**, and it typically carries a **lower points threshold than F-2-7** in exchange for a commitment to work and live in a designated area.

> [!KEY] The trade you are being offered
> F-2-R exchanges *geography* for *eligibility*. If you would clear F-2-7 comfortably, take F-2-7 and live wherever you like. If you are short on points and open to Busan, Ulsan, Changwon, Gumi or a designated regional area, F-2-R may get you to residence status years earlier — with lower housing costs as a side effect. The constraint is real, though: check the length and conditions of the residence commitment before you commit to it.

## 5. e-visa processing with AI screening

A **simplified application process** now lets applicants use an **e-visa portal with AI-assisted screening** instead of attending an overseas Korean embassy in person.

For applicants in countries with one consulate and a three-month appointment queue, this removes what was often the longest delay in the whole process.

> [!ACTION] What automated screening means for your paperwork
> Machine pre-screening is unforgiving in a way human officers are not. Before you upload anything:
> - Make your **name identical** across passport, degree certificate, employment letter and application — including middle names, hyphens and romanisation.
> - Make **employment dates match exactly** between your CV, your reference letters and the form.
> - Use **apostilled or consular-legalised** documents where required, not scans of uncertified copies.
> - Keep **file names clean and descriptive**. It costs nothing and avoids mismatches.
> A single inconsistency that a human would have queried by email can trigger an automated rejection.

## How the routes compare

| Route | Tied to employer? | Best for | Standout feature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Top-Tier** | No | Senior professionals, researchers, academics | Parents and domestic helper eligible |
| **E-7-1** | Yes | Professional and technical hires with an offer | Experience waiver via KCCI programme |
| **E-7-M (K-CORE)** | Yes | Graduates of Korean technical junior colleges | Defined route to long-term residence |
| **F-2-7** | No | Anyone who can clear the points threshold | Employer-independent residence |
| **F-2-R** | No | People willing to live in a designated region | Lower points threshold |

## The strategy most people should follow

If you are arriving on an employer-tied visa, treat it as a **staging post, not a destination**. Employer-tied status is fragile: a redundancy, a company restructuring or a soured relationship becomes an immigration problem rather than a job-hunting problem.

From the week you land:

1. **Read the F-2-7 points table** and work out your current score.
2. **Book a TOPIK sitting.** Korean language is among the largest movable components of your score, and it takes time.
3. **Keep every document** — contracts, tax certificates, income statements, degree certificates. You will need years of them, and reconstructing them later is painful.
4. **Recheck the rules annually.** 2026 was a year of expansion; the direction has not always been this way, and policy that widened can narrow.

## Why Korea is doing this

None of this is generosity. Korea's fertility rate is recovering from a record low but remains far below replacement, the working-age population is contracting, and regional industrial cities are losing people faster than the national average. Skilled migration is one of the few levers available on a policy-relevant timescale. We covered the underlying numbers in [what Korea's 2026 birth data actually shows](/society/korea-birth-rate-2026-rebound/).

That context is useful to hold, because it tells you which direction the policy pressure runs. Routes tied to **regional residence** and to **manufacturing skills** are the ones aligned with the state's actual problem — and are therefore the ones most likely to be widened further rather than quietly tightened.
